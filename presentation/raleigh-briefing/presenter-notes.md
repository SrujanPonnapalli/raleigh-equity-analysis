## The central claim

The broad completed-request comparison shows little linear association between
ZIP income and recorded resolution time. Parks income comparisons also change
with the areas retained. A local screen identifies a persistent recorded-resolution
shortfall of 10.9–15.9 percentage points in 27610 relative to the other 14 analysis
ZIPs. A specified Atlas follow-up does not establish a consistent broader
vulnerability pattern. The actionable request is a focused records and process
review; the cause and physical-work status remain unverified.

The 27610 demographic profile is descriptive context added after the local
screen. It did not select the flag and does not test racial disparities in
service. Use the 2020–2024 ACS figures and explicit race/ethnicity definitions,
not the mixed-year Google summary. A static profile cannot establish growth,
gentrification, or displacement.

## Suggested narration

### 1. Wealth & Wait Times

0:00–0:35. The title asks the project’s original question. We examine recorded service-request outcomes, not a causal income effect. Ask Raleigh is the saved source; the broader 311 label is the presentation framing. This briefing covers the full repository: broad ZIP comparisons, parks follow-up, a local investigation screen, and the specified Atlas extension. Presenter details remain blank.

### 2. The project question

0:35–1:05. Introduce the three evidence sources and the distinction between reported need and actual need. Census income and vulnerability describe areas, not the identity of requesters or park users. Explain that the project narrows scope as comparisons become more defensible.

### 3. Data and scope

1:05–1:40. The refreshed snapshot contains 18,526 requests across services. The local investigation screen narrows to 773 Parks & Recreation submissions from July 1, 2025 through April 30, 2026 in the original 15 analysis ZIPs. All have enough elapsed time for a 180-day endpoint. The broad scatter separately uses 13,117 completed requests matched to 15 ZIP incomes. These are nested counts, not totals to add together. A resident who never submits a request is absent from this data.

### 4. The broad ZIP comparison

1:40–2:25. This is the repository’s broad completed-request comparison. The 15 actual ZIP averages have Pearson r = −0.059 (about −0.06), with exploratory p about .834. The plot is one point per ZIP, not 13,117 independent income observations. It mixes services and omits requests lacking a resolution timestamp; it cannot establish equal service or answer the unfinished-request question. We do not treat this correlation as the headline.

### 5. Compare like with like

2:25–3:10. A completed-only average hides requests that never receive a resolution timestamp. Here every eligible submission contributes to the denominator. We compare within service/request-type/month strata, then compute weighted averages using the same pooled task mix for both sides. This is traditional direct standardization. It improves comparability on recorded variables; it cannot control for unrecorded complexity, jurisdiction, or urgency.

### 6. Income sensitivity

3:10–3:55. The income comparison is a separate, overlapping cohort: July 2025 through July 2026, three ZIPs per income group. Raw 90-day rates are 47.5% lower-income versus 56.6% higher-income, a 9.1-point shortfall. Restricting to 447 common-support cases gives 6.1 points; weighting the same subset gives 4.7. Do not attribute the entire reduction to adjustment. Leave-one-ZIP-out gaps span −21.1 to +0.8; dropping 27613 changes direction. This is a sensitivity range, not a confidence interval. The six ZIP income estimates also carry ACS uncertainty. No stable broad income-disparity claim follows.

### 7. The local screening rule

3:55–4:35. We committed the local rule before running this screen, after initial exploration. It is not a preregistered or independent confirmatory study. The local rule requires 30 eligible focal requests per period, 80% focal coverage, and at least a 10-point deficit in each of four comparisons. Each stratum needs at least one focal and five reference cases. One ZIP meets it, two have enough data but do not meet it, twelve have insufficient comparable data. This is not an equity ranking of all ZIPs, and the 10-point threshold is not an SLA or a statistical significance cutoff.

### 8. 27610 in context

4:35–5:25. Introduce 27610 before its detailed service results. It includes parts of Southeast Raleigh, but its ZCTA extends beyond the city boundary. All context uses the 2020–2024 ACS release, not mixed years. Median household income is $67,932 ± $6,108 versus Raleigh $85,395 ± $2,159. Poverty is 15.7% ±2.7 points versus 11.9% ±0.8. Black alone is 54.7% ±3.2 versus 26.6% ±0.7; Hispanic or Latino of any race is 20.1% ±2.7 versus 12.6% ±0.7. These are 90% MOEs. Black race and Hispanic ethnicity overlap and must not be summed. The profile was added after the local screen; it did not select the flag. No racial service-outcome analysis has been performed. Do not infer that the requesters have these characteristics or that race caused the resolution pattern. A single period cannot establish population growth or gentrification.

### 9. The local shortfall

5:25–5:55. State the main result immediately. “Eleven to sixteen percentage points” is a difference between adjusted resolution proportions; it is not a percentage increase in wait time. The 27610 comparison is against the other 14 ZIPs in the analysis, not all of Raleigh. Broader socioeconomic checks do not establish a consistent disparity. None of these results prove inequitable treatment or physical work left undone.

### 10. Four local comparisons

5:55–6:50. Read the chart left to right. Red circles are 27610 and blue squares are the other 14 analysis ZIPs, adjusted to the same task/month mix for each comparison. First period: 46.3% versus 57.6% within 90 days, and 52.8% versus 63.7% within 180 days. Later period: 56.6% versus 68.9%, then 56.6% versus 72.4%. The shortfall is 10.9–15.9 points. Focal coverage is 84.7% and 82.5%; results apply only to retained common-support requests. Windows share cases and each comparison uses its own eligible weights. Do not interpret cross-period level changes as a controlled time trend.

### 11. 46 records to review

6:50–7:30. The flag points to an auditable set of cases. Across all 99 eligible 27610 parks requests, 46 remain New with no resolution timestamp: 26 Tree Service, 15 Park and Greenway Service, 5 Playgrounds. This is the raw full-cohort case count, not the adjusted common-support sample. There are 84 distinct address strings among the 99; tickets may repeat the same underlying problem. Do not call these 46 outstanding repairs or publish ticket addresses on the slides. Also sample resolved records so the review can validate both sides of the outcome definition.

### 12. What resolved means

7:30–8:10. This is the central operational uncertainty. Saved metadata define a resolution date but do not establish physical completion. Ticket status, administrative closure, referral, and work orders may follow different workflows. We have not contacted staff or linked work orders, so the ticket-to-work-order relationship needs confirmation. A work order might remain active after ticket closure, or finish while a ticket stays open; staff can clarify which situations occur. In the full snapshot, 52 records have closure before resolution, two before submission, and nine nonterminal-status records have resolution timestamps. These exceptions motivate clarifying definitions; they do not prove mismanagement.

### 13. Atlas geography

8:10–8:50. We tested whether ZIP income masked a clearer pattern at finer geography. Requests were joined strictly to county block-group polygons; no ZIP or nearest-area imputation. The join was checked before outcomes were examined, and a separate outcome plan was committed before vulnerability differences were calculated. Five indicators are poverty, unemployment, age dependency, vacancy, and education. Of 773 parks cases, 761 are usable across 201 block groups. The median group has three cases, too few for credible individual block-group performance claims. Higher bands 4–5 are compared with lower 1–2; middle band 3 is excluded. This pilot remains exploratory, because service and snapshot had already been chosen.

### 14. Atlas outcome checks

8:50–9:35. Primary adjusted rates are 55.1% in higher-vulnerability areas and 59.4% in lower, a 4.3-point deficit. But the 90-day gap changes from −7.9 in the first period to +1.9 in the second. Poverty alone gives +1.6 and compares different groups. The stricter five-per-cell comparison retains 463 cases and yields −3.8. The gate for another lead required at least a 10-point primary shortfall plus consistent directions and coverage conditions; it fails. The 180-day pooled gap is −5.8 and all specified checks are in Appendix C. Leave-one-block-group-out stays negative, but that does not resolve instability by time and measure. We stopped this specified comparison; it did not create a second actionable lead.

### 15. What the records leave out

9:35–10:10. Keep these limits connected to action. Reporting data cannot measure unmet need or whether some residents face higher reporting barriers. A park location does not identify which residents benefit from it, and neighborhood rankings do not identify an individual’s income. Current municipal jurisdiction and unrecorded work complexity were not independently established. We do not report adjusted significance tests or causal estimates. The lack of a consistent broad pattern is not evidence that services are equitable; it sets the scope of what this data can support.

### 16. Proposed city review

10:10–11:05. Ask for a limited collaborative review rather than a new large study. Review 46 New cases and a sample of resolved cases to validate the outcome definition, deduplicate reports, assess routing and jurisdiction, and link work orders. Agree on operational definitions and an owner for each case. Depending on the evidence, fixes may involve record updates, workflow clarification, routing, or outstanding work. These are possible responses, not diagnosed failures. After validation, use the fixed scope and comparisons for a follow-up; avoid searching new cuts of the same snapshot for a more dramatic result.

### 17. Discussion

11:05–11:45, leaving time for discussion within a 10–15 minute slot. Ask whether the parks and service-request teams can review the 46 New records and a sample of resolved cases, and clarify how ticket resolution relates to completed work. The persistent 27610 gap supports that review. Its cause and resident impact are still unknown. Use the appendix for methodological questions.

### 18. Calculation

Questions: Why no regression? Direct standardization is transparent and sufficient for the descriptive question on common support. Each group’s stratum-specific resolution rate is averaged with common pooled weights. The contrast does not account for all severity or workflow differences. Requests can be repeated or correlated within location. No request-independent binomial p-values or adjusted confidence intervals are supplied, so do not use “statistically significant.” The 90- and 180-day windows use the same requests. Missing resolution is an outcome within the window, not a reason to discard a case.

### 19. Local screen details

All four rates and signs are shown for auditability. ZIP list shorthand includes 27601, 27603, 27604, 27605, 27606, 27607, 27608, 27609, 27610, 27612, 27613, 27614, 27615, 27616, 27617. Group reference is other 14 analysis ZIPs. Focal common-support coverage is reported, and no result is projected to excluded cases. The rule is for selecting an investigation, not ranking equity or testing a causal effect. Frozen requests and committed policy/results support reproducibility.

### 20. All Atlas checks

The primary contrast excludes 110 middle-band requests from 761 usable parks matches, leaving 651 eligible lower/higher-band cases. Common support retains 274 lower plus 356 higher, total 630. Pooled bands 1–2 versus 4–5 follow county definitions. Poverty sensitivity uses county positive-population 40th and 60th percentile cutoffs, ties retained together; it defines different groups. Gate also required 80% coverage, 30 per group in each period, consistent 90-day direction in both periods and poverty sensitivity, and negative leave-one-block-group-out gaps. Failing the gate does not prove no inequities. Atlas source tie handling is undocumented; equal indicator values can receive different component ranks, and no MOEs are supplied.

### 21. Sources

Source links point to the public datasets. The local frozen snapshots, metadata, scripts, policy commits, and CSV outputs allow the results to be inspected. Do not silently replace snapshot-specific charts with later live data. The current public service may change after November 10. The Atlas provides geographic context, not a verified municipal service boundary. Read the accompanying project README and outcome documents for exact denominators and full limitations.

### 22. Census context details

The context compares a ZCTA, city, and county, all from one five-year ACS release. These geographic areas overlap and the slide does not claim a formal significance test. Census source rows, full-download and subset hashes, approximate 90% MOEs, and denominator definitions are saved under data/presentation_context. Derived proportion MOEs use Census subset formulas and a ratio fallback if needed; negative sentinel codes are never treated as numeric MOEs. The map uses Census ACS2024 polygons, with 2020 ZCTA delineation and small simplification for orientation. It does not define current Raleigh service jurisdiction. Growth, displacement, diversity-index rankings, age, housing values, and rent-burden claims from the proposed background were not added as study findings.
